## Tips to check

These are the only new words in Key sounds. Each is summarised from the quoted source. Please read each one and say if it is fine.

**Tones**

> 1st tone stays high and level. 2nd rises. 3rd is low: it starts lower than the 2nd, or about where the 2nd starts, and usually only rises again before a pause. 4th falls from high. The 3rd often sounds a little creaky.

Source: Wikipedia, Standard Chinese phonology (CC BY-SA 4.0): contours 55, 35, 21(4), 51; "The third tone starts lower or around the starting point for the second tone"; the rise "is normally heard only if it appears at the end of a sentence or before a pause"; "third tone is usually pronounced with creaky voice"

**zh ch sh · z c s · j q x**

> zh ch sh are said with the tongue tip turned back (retroflex). z is like ts in cats, c is the same with a puff of air, and s is like English s, usually with the tongue on the lower teeth. j q x are a third set, further back: j is like an English ch with no puff of air.

Source: Wikipedia, Standard Chinese phonology (CC BY-SA 4.0): zh "Similar to ch in English chat, but with a retroflex articulation and no aspiration"; z "Like English ts in cats, without aspiration"; s "Like English s, but usually with the tongue on the lower teeth"; j "Like an unaspirated English ch, but with an alveolo-palatal pronunciation". "Tongue tip turned back" is our plain-English gloss of retroflex.

**Puffs of air**

> In Chinese, b d g and p t k differ by a puff of air, not by voicing as in English. b d g have no puff: like the p in spy, the t in sty, the k in scar. p t k have the puff. The same goes for z/c, zh/ch and j/q.

Source: Wikipedia, Standard Chinese phonology (CC BY-SA 4.0): "the primary distinction is not voiced vs. voiceless" but "unaspirated vs. aspirated"; b "Like English p but unaspirated – as in spy", d "as in sty", g "as in scar"; c "as z, but with aspiration"

**ü**

> ü is like French u or German ü. English has no vowel like it. After j, q and x it is written u, so jù and qù have ü too.

Source: Wikipedia, Standard Chinese phonology (CC BY-SA 4.0): ü "Like French u or German ü"; "ü is written as u after j, q, or x"

**-n / -ng**

> A Chinese syllable can only end in a consonant if that consonant is n or ng, so this ending is often the only thing that tells two words apart.

Source: Wikipedia, Standard Chinese phonology (CC BY-SA 4.0): "the only consonants that can appear in syllable coda (final) position are /n/ and /ŋ/"
